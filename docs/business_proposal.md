## Executive Summary

Retail businesses do not have a shortage of data. They have a shortage of **explainable intelligence**.

A dashboard can tell a business leader that revenue dropped by 18% this week. It can visualize the decline, highlight the affected KPI, and show historical trends. But it cannot reliably answer the questions that matter next:

- **Why did it happen?**
- **Where did the impact originate?**
- **Which explanation is actually supported by evidence?**
- **How confident should we be in that explanation?**
- **What should the business do next?**

We built the **KPI Intelligence-to-Action Engine** to close this gap.

The system detects statistically significant KPI movements, traces them to the stores or departments driving the change, retrieves relevant operational evidence such as tickets and field reports, evaluates multiple competing explanations, and produces a confidence-aware diagnosis.

Most importantly, **it knows when not to answer**.

If the available evidence is insufficient or contradictory, the system explicitly abstains instead of generating a plausible-sounding explanation. Once a diagnosis is established, it converts the result into a structured recommendation with an appropriate owner and communication style for the intended business audience.

The diagnostic pipeline is deliberately designed so that **a language model is never the source of a number or the authority behind a diagnosis**.

The entire diagnostic core operates deterministically, with a live telemetry and audit layer that makes this claim measurable rather than theoretical.

# 1. The Problem

## Data Exists Everywhere. Answers Do Not.

Retail organizations operate across multiple systems with different data sources, refresh cycles, and definitions.

For example:

- Point-of-sale data may refresh daily.
- Pricing and markdown data may refresh weekly.
- Operational information may exist inside tickets.
- Field observations may be recorded as free-text notes.
- External factors such as weather, staffing shortages, or competitor activity may exist outside traditional BI systems.

These systems contain valuable information, but they rarely work together as a single investigative workflow.

When a KPI changes significantly, an analyst typically has to:

1. Detect that something unusual happened.
2. Identify where the change originated.
3. Search multiple systems for potential explanations.
4. Determine which explanations are actually supported.
5. Assess confidence in the conclusion.
6. Decide what action should be taken.
7. Communicate the result differently to different stakeholders.

This process can take **one to three days** and depends heavily on analyst availability.

### The Existing Alternatives Have a Fundamental Gap

A traditional dashboard solves only the first part of the problem.

It tells you:

> **"What happened?"**

But not:

> **"Why did it happen, how certain are we, and what should we do?"**

A generic AI chatbot has the opposite problem.

It can produce a natural-language explanation quickly, but an explanation generated by an LLM is not necessarily an explanation supported by the underlying evidence.

An AI system that always produces an answer can be more dangerous than one that sometimes refuses to answer.

The missing capability is therefore not another visualization or another chatbot.

It is a system that can **reason from evidence and quantify its own confidence**.

# 2. Our Solution

## From KPI Monitoring to Intelligence-to-Action

The KPI Intelligence-to-Action Engine is built around one core principle:

> **A language model is never allowed to be the source of a number or the authority behind a diagnosis.**

The system separates **computation, evidence retrieval, reasoning, confidence assessment, and communication** into distinct stages.

This creates a controlled pipeline in which every important conclusion can be traced back to measurable evidence.

---

## How the System Works

The diagnostic workflow consists of five major stages.

### 1. Detect

The system first determines whether a KPI movement is actually significant.

A statistical layer evaluates the movement using:

- Control-limit checks
- Seasonal decomposition
- Historical behavior
- Business-impact thresholds

A KPI does not enter the diagnostic pipeline simply because it moved.

The movement must be both:

- **Statistically meaningful**, and
- **Material enough in financial terms to matter**

This prevents the system from wasting investigative effort on normal fluctuations.

---

### 2. Decompose

Once a KPI movement is confirmed as material, the system determines where the movement originated.

The deterministic aggregation layer identifies:

- Which store contributed to the movement
- Which department contributed
- How much each entity contributed to the overall change

This stage is pure computation.

There is no generative inference involved.

For example:

> Revenue decreased by $100K.

The decomposition layer may determine:

> Store 12 contributed $61K of the decline, while Department A accounted for $38K of that store-level movement.

This provides the investigative scope for the next stage.

---

### 3. Retrieve

After identifying the affected business area, the system searches for relevant operational evidence.

A **hybrid retrieval layer** combines:

- Keyword matching
- Semantic embedding search
- Contextual filtering

Instead of searching the entire archive indiscriminately, the system focuses on evidence connected to the relevant:

- Store
- Department
- KPI
- Time period
- Business context

This can include:

- Operational tickets
- Field reports
- Business notes
- Incident records

The objective is simple:

> **Retrieve evidence that could explain the observed movement.**

---

### 4. Reason

This is the core intelligence layer.

Instead of selecting the first plausible explanation, the system maintains multiple competing hypotheses and evaluates each against the available evidence.

For every candidate explanation, the system considers both:

- Evidence **supporting** the hypothesis
- Evidence **contradicting** the hypothesis

This distinction is critical.

If a ticket supports a possible cause, the hypothesis gains strength.

If another ticket directly contradicts that cause, the hypothesis is downgraded.

The system therefore does not simply search for evidence that confirms its initial assumption.

It actively attempts to **disprove competing explanations**.

This makes the reasoning process closer to an analytical investigation than a conventional generative-AI response.

---

### 5. Know Its Limits

A diagnosis is only useful if the business knows how much it should trust it.

The system therefore calculates a structured confidence score using three independent signals:

1. **Evidence consistency**  
   How well the retrieved evidence agrees with itself.

2. **Retrieval strength**  
   How strongly the retrieved evidence matches the specific investigation.

3. **Reasoning stability**  
   Whether the same conclusion remains consistent when the investigation is evaluated through multiple query formulations.

These signals determine whether the system should:

- Commit to a diagnosis, or
- Abstain from making one.

When the system abstains, it does not simply return:

> "Insufficient information."

It explains **why confidence is low** and identifies what additional evidence would help resolve the ambiguity.

---

# 3. The Role of the Language Model

The language model is intentionally placed at the **end of the pipeline**, rather than at the center of the reasoning process.

Its responsibility is limited to:

> **Turning already-established facts into clear, natural language.**

The model receives the facts, diagnosis, confidence information, and recommendation that have already been determined by the system.

It is explicitly instructed not to introduce facts that were not provided to it.

### Why This Matters

The LLM does not decide:

- The KPI value
- The magnitude of the change
- Which store caused the change
- Which evidence is valid
- Which hypothesis wins
- Whether the evidence is sufficient

Those decisions happen before the language model is involved.

If the LLM is unavailable, the system automatically falls back to a deterministic, template-based response containing the same underlying facts.

We also tested this failure mode during development against a genuine API billing interruption, and the diagnostic system continued operating without interruption.

# 4. Why This Approach?

We considered two simpler approaches and deliberately rejected both.

---

## Option 1: Build a Better Dashboard

A dashboard extension could provide:

- Better visualizations
- More filters
- Faster queries
- Additional KPI breakdowns

This would improve visibility.

But it would not solve the core problem.

A dashboard can show **what changed**, but it cannot reliably establish **why it changed** without connecting the KPI to external operational evidence.

---

## Option 2: Put an LLM on Top of the Data

The second option was to simply pass the KPI data and relevant context to an LLM and ask:

> "Why did this KPI change?"

This approach is fast to build and produces impressive demos.

However, it introduces a fundamental risk.

An LLM is optimized to produce a useful response. If the evidence is weak, it can still generate a plausible explanation.

That creates a dangerous failure mode:

> **A confident explanation without sufficient evidence.**

Even asking the model to "be confident only when appropriate" does not solve the underlying problem if the model itself is responsible for judging its own confidence.

---

## The Middle Path

Our system deliberately takes a harder approach.

| Responsibility | System Component |
|---|---|
| Detect KPI anomalies | Statistical layer |
| Calculate impact | Deterministic aggregation |
| Find relevant evidence | Hybrid retrieval |
| Test explanations | Evidence-based reasoning |
| Measure confidence | Independent confidence model |
| Decide whether to answer | Abstention logic |
| Communicate the result | Language model |

The language model therefore acts as a **communication layer**, not a decision-making layer.

# 5. What We Actually Built

This is not a concept-only architecture.

Every major component described above exists as working, tested code.

The prototype has been validated against real data and real investigative scenarios rather than purely illustrative examples.

## Real Data

The system operates on:

- **421,570 retail transaction records**
- **45 stores**
- **81 departments**
- Operational tickets
- Field reports

The system also manages **five connected KPIs** through a shared semantic contract.

This contract defines:

- KPI formulas
- Thresholds
- Data lineage
- Relevant business definitions

The objective is to ensure that the system is reasoning over governed KPI definitions rather than treating each number as an isolated metric.

# 6. Demonstration Scenarios

The prototype covers the five required demonstration scenarios.

### Scenario 1 — Single-Cause Diagnosis

A store experiences a **53% revenue decline**.

The system:

1. Detects the statistically significant movement.
2. Decomposes the impact.
3. Retrieves relevant operational evidence.
4. Identifies a weather-driven supply disruption.
5. Corroborates the explanation using four independent tickets.
6. Produces a high-confidence diagnosis.

---

### Scenario 2 — Multi-Factor Diagnosis

A second store experiences a KPI movement with several plausible explanations.

Three competing hypotheses are identified.

The system does not immediately select the most obvious one.

Instead, it evaluates the evidence and discovers that one candidate explanation is directly contradicted by an operational ticket.

That hypothesis is therefore downgraded, allowing the system to identify the stronger underlying driver.

---

### Scenario 3 — Contradictory Evidence

In another scenario, the retrieved evidence contains conflicting information.

Rather than forcing a conclusion, the system recognizes that the evidence does not support a sufficiently confident diagnosis.

It therefore **abstains**.

This is an intentional system behavior, not an error.

The system communicates that additional evidence is required before a reliable conclusion can be made.

---

### Scenario 4 — Sparse Historical Data

The system also handles cases where sufficient historical data does not exist.

For a newly launched department, the system uses an appropriate peer cohort for comparison while applying a reduced confidence ceiling.

For a one-off product pilot where no valid comparison exists, the system refuses to fabricate a baseline.

This ensures that a lack of historical data does not become an excuse to manufacture certainty.

---

### Scenario 5 — Role-Based Access

The same underlying investigation can produce different views depending on the user's authorization.

For example:

- A regional manager outside the relevant territory is denied access.
- A manager with appropriate territory access receives a redacted view.
- HR can access the complete information available to its role.

This demonstrates that evidence-based intelligence must also respect organizational access boundaries.

# 7. Determinism and Auditability

The determinism of the diagnostic pipeline is not simply a design claim.

It is measured through telemetry.

Every investigation is logged and each step is classified as either:

- **Deterministic**
- **LLM-driven**

Across the test runs, **100% of the diagnostic pipeline registers as deterministic**.

The only language-model interaction is used for narrative phrasing after the diagnosis has already been established.

This gives us an auditable distinction between:

> **What the system calculated**

and

> **What the language model said about it.**

That separation is fundamental to the trust model of the solution.

The value of the system changes depending on who is using it.

---

## For a Store Manager

The system turns a KPI alert into an actionable instruction.

Instead of:

> "Revenue is down 53%."

The manager receives:

- What happened
- Why it happened
- How confident the system is
- What action should be taken

This allows leadership to prioritize the issues that actually require attention.

## For the Organization

The broader organizational impact is the reduction of manual investigation time.

A process that can traditionally take **one to three days** can instead run in seconds.

More importantly, the output is not simply another dashboard notification.

It becomes one of two things:

### A confident, evidence-backed recommendation

**or**

### An honest "not yet"

when the evidence does not justify a conclusion.

That distinction is central to building trustworthy business intelligence.

# 9. Architecture Principles

The prototype is built around several architectural principles.

### Deterministic First

Numbers, thresholds, calculations, and KPI decomposition are handled through deterministic logic.

### Evidence Before Explanation

The system retrieves and evaluates evidence before generating a narrative.

### Competing Hypotheses

The reasoning layer does not stop at the first plausible explanation.

### Contradiction Matters

Evidence that disproves a hypothesis is treated as first-class information.

### Confidence Is Computed

Confidence is derived from measurable signals rather than being generated by the language model.

### Abstention Is a Feature

When evidence is insufficient, the system is designed to refuse rather than guess.

### LLM as a Communication Layer

The language model improves readability and audience-specific communication without becoming the source of truth.

### Auditability

Every investigation can be traced through telemetry to understand how the final result was produced.

# 10. Current Limitations

We believe in being explicit about what the prototype does not yet do.

The current system is tuned to the schema of the dataset used for this implementation.

While the core architecture — including:

- Reasoning
- Confidence scoring
- Security enforcement
- Recommendation generation

is not fundamentally tied to this dataset, several modules still reference dataset-specific column names directly.

The next step is to make this binding fully dynamic through the governed semantic layer.

This would allow the engine to be connected to a different company's dataset without requiring code changes to the core diagnostic logic.

---

## Forecasting and Formal Causal Inference

The current system does not yet include:

- Forecasting
- A formal causal-inference model
- Counterfactual analysis

The existing reasoning framework evaluates and eliminates hypotheses against observed evidence.

This represents meaningful evidence-based causal reasoning in practice, but it should not be confused with a formal causal model capable of counterfactual inference.

---

## Natural-Language Querying

The current prototype does not yet provide unrestricted free-text investigation such as:

> "Why did Store 12 drop last week?"

This is a natural next step.

Importantly, this addition would primarily affect **query interpretation**, rather than the diagnostic engine itself.

The existing evidence, reasoning, confidence, and abstention mechanisms could remain unchanged.

---

# 11. What's Next

The next evolution of the system is focused on making the existing architecture more general and scalable.

### 1. Fully Dynamic Semantic Binding

Remove dataset-specific column dependencies and allow KPI definitions to be resolved entirely through the governed semantic layer.

### 2. Natural-Language Investigation

Allow business users to ask questions directly in natural language while mapping those questions into the existing deterministic diagnostic pipeline.

### 3. Forecasting

Extend the engine from explaining current KPI movements to identifying potential future deviations.

### 4. Formal Causal Inference

Introduce counterfactual and causal models where the business problem requires stronger causal guarantees.

### 5. Broader Enterprise Integration

Extend the architecture to additional enterprise data sources while preserving the same evidence-first and confidence-aware design principles.

# 12. Closing

The easiest version of this problem is also the easiest one to demo:

> Put a language model on top of some data and let it explain what happened.

We deliberately chose not to build that.

We built a system that:

- Tests its own conclusions
- Evaluates competing explanations
- Searches for contradictory evidence
- Quantifies confidence
- Refuses to guess when evidence is insufficient
- Keeps the diagnostic pipeline deterministic
- Uses AI only where it adds value without compromising the underlying truth

The result is not simply an AI-powered dashboard.

It is an **intelligence-to-action engine** designed to move a business from:

> **"Something changed."**

to:

> **"This is what changed, this is why we believe it happened, this is how confident we are, and this is what should happen next."**

And when the evidence is not strong enough:

> **"We don't know yet — and here is what we need to find out."**

That is the foundation of trustworthy AI for business intelligence.
